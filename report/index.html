Method and limits
Sampling
Reading every block of a day through the public RPC takes most of a day, so the scan reads short runs of consecutive blocks spread evenly over 24 hours. Because the windows are spread evenly in time, the share of transactions per label is a fair estimate of the day. Daily totals are scaled up from the sample.
Wallet counts lean busy
A wallet that sends every few seconds is almost certain to appear in a 30-second window; a person who visits once a day usually is not. So wallet counts over-represent busy wallets. Read the transaction shares as the headline, and treat no-signal wallet counts as a floor.
Signals
Lifetime transaction count comes from the wallet's nonce on the RPC. Hours active, bursts and repeated calls come from the sample. A shared funding wallet is not used: almost every wallet, person or script, was funded by the faucet.
Matching apps
An address found in exactly one app's website code, on its homepage or a page linked from it, belongs to that app. Contracts from the same deployer inherit it. Otherwise a contract or token name that contains the app's name. Addresses several apps embed, such as shared tokens, stay unmatched. Sites behind a bot check were not scanned. — of — listed apps gave up contract addresses this way.
Who runs the scripts
Scripted wallets calling an app do not mean the app runs them. Apps with airdrops, points or staking rewards attract farmers who script their own wallets. This scan shows where the scripted traffic goes, not who sent it.
What this cannot tell
There is no confirmed list of which wallets are bots, so these are behaviour labels, not verdicts. A careful script with random delays will pass as no signals. A person who batches many actions quickly can trip the burst signal.
